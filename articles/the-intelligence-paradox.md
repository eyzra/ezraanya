Imagine a young professional who uses ChatGPT to write emails, summarise reports, develop presentations, analyse business problems, and prepare for meetings. In one working day, he accomplishes what might previously have taken him three. His employer is pleased. His productivity has improved, and he has more time to attend to other responsibilities.

Six months later, he is asked to prepare a strategic recommendation without AI. He struggles to organise his thoughts, develop an argument, and arrive at a defensible conclusion.

Has he become more intelligent because he can accomplish more, or has he become more dependent on a system that does much of his thinking for him?

This is one of the most important questions emerging from the artificial intelligence revolution, and I suspect we are paying far less attention to it than we should.

For years, the conversation around AI has revolved around productivity. How much faster can we work? How many tasks can we automate? How much money can businesses save? These are legitimate questions, particularly for organisations under pressure to improve performance.

But there is another question worth investigating: ***what happens to human intelligence when the effort required to think becomes increasingly optional?***

In 2025, researchers from Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon University surveyed 319 knowledge workers about their use of generative AI. They collected 936 examples of AI-assisted tasks and found that greater confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical thinking. Interestingly, workers who had greater confidence in their own abilities reported more critical thinking.

The study did not establish that AI was making people less intelligent. It revealed something worth examining: the relationship between our confidence in technology and the effort we invest in our own thinking.

To understand why this matters, we need to look at how human intelligence develops, what AI is doing to the process, and what we might be sacrificing in our pursuit of efficiency.

### The difference between getting answers and developing intelligence

Think about the last time you struggled to understand a difficult concept.

Perhaps it was a mathematical problem, a philosophical argument, or a complicated business decision. You read through the material, tried different approaches, made mistakes, and eventually arrived at an understanding.

Something happened during that process beyond finding the correct answer.

You developed your capacity to reason through similar problems.

Psychologists have studied this phenomenon for decades. One relevant concept is *desirable difficulties*, associated with the work of cognitive psychologist Robert Bjork. The idea is that certain difficulties during learning can improve long-term retention and transfer, even when they make the immediate learning experience feel slower.

This explains why attempting to recall something from memory can be more beneficial than repeatedly reading the answer. It also helps explain why working through a problem may produce more durable learning than watching someone else solve it.

Now introduce AI into the picture.

A student who previously spent forty minutes working through a difficult problem can receive a complete explanation in seconds. A marketer struggling to develop a campaign strategy can generate ten possible directions with a single prompt. A manager can ask AI to analyse a business problem and recommend a course of action.

The immediate result may be impressive. The user gets something useful, often much faster.

But the intellectual benefit depends on what happens next.

Does the student examine the explanation and attempt the problem independently? Does the marketer evaluate the assumptions behind the suggested strategies? Does the manager understand why one recommendation is better than another?

There is a meaningful difference between possessing an answer and developing the capacity to produce or evaluate one.

And that distinction becomes increasingly important when answers are cheap.

### The productivity numbers are impressive. The learning numbers are more complicated.

We have good reasons to be excited about AI's economic potential.

In a study published in *The Quarterly Journal of Economics* in 2025, researchers Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond examined the introduction of a generative AI assistant among 5,172 customer-support agents.

Access to AI increased productivity, measured by issues resolved per hour, by an average of 15%. The benefits varied considerably between workers, with less experienced employees generally gaining more. The researchers also found suggestive evidence that AI helped newer workers learn effective practices from more experienced colleagues.

This is important because it challenges the assumption that AI assistance necessarily weakens human competence. In some environments, AI may help people acquire knowledge and perform at levels that previously required years of experience.

Now consider a different study.

In 2025, researcher André Barcaui conducted a randomised controlled trial involving 120 undergraduate students learning about artificial intelligence. One group used ChatGPT as a study aid, while another used traditional study methods.

Forty-five days later, the students took an unexpected knowledge-retention test.

The traditional study group scored 68.5%, compared with 57.5% for the ChatGPT-assisted group, a difference of 11 percentage points.

The researchers concluded that unrestricted ChatGPT use may undermine long-term retention by reducing the mental effort involved in learning. This was one study with a particular group of students, so we should be careful about generalising its findings to every learning environment.

Still, the two studies raise an interesting possibility.

AI can improve immediate performance while producing different effects on the knowledge people develop along the way.

We should therefore be asking two questions whenever we introduce AI into a learning or working environment.

How much better does it help people perform today? And what capabilities are they developing that will remain useful tomorrow?

### The hidden cost of cognitive offloading

Human beings have always found ways to reduce the mental effort required to accomplish tasks.

We use calculators for arithmetic, calendars to remember appointments, maps to navigate unfamiliar places, and search engines to retrieve information.

Psychologists call this *cognitive offloading*: using external tools or processes to reduce the demands placed on our internal cognitive resources.

It is one reason we can accomplish complex tasks without keeping every piece of information in our heads.

Consider a pilot using instruments to navigate an aircraft. The instruments provide information that would be difficult or impossible to obtain through human perception alone. Their usefulness depends partly on the pilot's ability to interpret them and respond appropriately.

AI introduces a more complicated form of offloading because it can participate in activities we traditionally associate with thinking itself.

It can construct arguments, generate hypotheses, interpret evidence, write explanations, and recommend decisions.

Suppose you are preparing a marketing strategy for a new business.

You could spend several hours researching customers, identifying competitors, examining buying behaviour, and developing possible positioning strategies.

Or you could describe the business to ChatGPT and ask it to produce a complete marketing strategy.

The second approach may save time. It might even produce a better initial document.

But imagine that the strategy fails after implementation.

Would you understand the assumptions that led to the recommendations? Could you identify where the analysis went wrong? Would you know which conclusions to revisit?

This is where the danger of excessive cognitive offloading becomes apparent.

When we outsource the intellectual process without understanding it, we may lose the ability to interrogate the result.

And in business, the cost of a poorly understood recommendation can be substantial.

### When confidence in AI replaces confidence in judgment

Let's return to the Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon study.

The researchers found that workers who trusted AI more tended to report less critical-thinking effort during AI-assisted tasks. They also observed that AI changed the nature of the thinking workers performed.

Instead of spending as much effort producing information themselves, workers increasingly focused on verifying, integrating, and supervising AI-generated material.

This shift deserves attention.

Imagine a financial analyst preparing an investment recommendation. Previously, she might have gathered information, constructed a model, evaluated competing assumptions, and developed her conclusion.

With AI, parts of that process can happen almost immediately.

Her responsibility then moves toward evaluating the model's output, checking assumptions, and determining whether the recommendation makes sense.

That can be a sophisticated form of work. In fact, it may require more judgment than producing the original calculations.

The problem emerges when the analyst lacks the expertise necessary to evaluate what the system produces.

A convincing explanation can create an illusion of understanding. A well-structured report can make weak reasoning appear credible. An authoritative tone can encourage us to accept conclusions we have barely examined.

I find this particularly interesting because it exposes a weakness in how we often measure intelligence at work.

We celebrate the person who produces the report fastest, generates the most ideas, or completes the greatest number of tasks.

Yet the person who can explain why a recommendation is wrong may be considerably more valuable than the person who generated it in thirty seconds.

The ability to recognise a bad answer becomes more important as the cost of producing plausible answers approaches zero.

### Even productivity can be an illusion

There is another complication in the assumption that AI always makes us more productive.

In July 2025, the research organisation METR published a randomised study examining how experienced open-source software developers performed when using AI tools.

The researchers studied 16 developers completing 246 tasks in software repositories they knew well.

Before beginning, the developers predicted that AI would reduce their task completion time by approximately 24%.

The actual result was surprising.

The developers completed their tasks 19% more slowly when allowed to use AI.

Even after the experiment, they believed AI had made them faster.

There are limitations to this finding. The study involved a small number of experienced developers, specific software projects, and AI tools available in early 2025. It would be unreasonable to conclude that AI slows down software development generally.

What interests me is the gap between perceived productivity and measured productivity.

We may feel productive because AI makes certain activities easier. We generate more material, receive faster responses, and experience less friction.

But producing more material is only one part of accomplishing valuable work.

A business can generate twenty marketing campaigns in an afternoon and still fail to understand its customers. A manager can produce detailed strategic documents without resolving the organisation's underlying problems. A student can complete assignments efficiently while retaining very little of what was studied.

The ease of producing something can distort our perception of how much we have accomplished.

And when that happens, productivity becomes difficult to distinguish from activity.

### What happens when the next generation learns to think with AI?

This is where the conversation becomes particularly important for education and the future of work.

Think about how people traditionally develop professional expertise.

A junior lawyer spends years researching cases, examining arguments, and drafting documents. A young designer learns by creating imperfect designs, receiving feedback, and improving them. A marketing associate develops judgment by studying customers, making recommendations, and observing what happens when those recommendations meet reality.

These activities involve mistakes, repetition, and the gradual accumulation of experience.

Now imagine that AI performs much of the preliminary work.

The lawyer receives a draft argument. The designer gets twenty design concepts. The marketer obtains a complete campaign proposal.

Their work may become more efficient, and the quality of their immediate output may improve.

But how will they develop the judgment required to supervise increasingly sophisticated AI systems?

An experienced professional can often recognise a poor recommendation because years of practice have developed their intuition. A beginner may accept the same recommendation because it sounds convincing.

This creates a possible problem for organisations.

If we automate too many of the activities through which beginners become experts, we may eventually struggle to produce the experts required to supervise the automation.

There is no reason to assume this outcome is inevitable. The customer-support study discussed earlier suggests AI can accelerate learning by making effective practices more accessible.

The important question is how we design the learning experience.

An AI assistant that explains its reasoning, challenges assumptions, and helps a junior employee work through a problem may contribute to professional development. A system that simply completes every task may produce a different outcome.

Organisations should therefore evaluate AI adoption partly by its effects on employee development.

A business that saves twenty hours of work this month but gradually weakens its employees' ability to solve unfamiliar problems may be creating a cost that will only become visible years later.

### Learning to think with machines

I believe the most useful response to these concerns is to become more deliberate about how we use AI.

For individuals, this begins with understanding the difference between using AI to complete a task and using AI to improve our ability to complete that task.

If you are learning a new concept, try explaining it yourself before asking AI to evaluate your understanding. Ask the system to identify weaknesses in your explanation and challenge you with questions.

If you are developing a business strategy, begin by articulating your own assumptions. Use AI to examine competing possibilities, introduce relevant evidence, and identify considerations you may have overlooked.

If you are writing an article, develop your central argument before using AI to test its logic, research its claims, or explore alternative interpretations.

And when AI produces something important, ask yourself whether you understand it well enough to defend it.

For organisations, the challenge extends beyond individual habits.

Managers need to think carefully about which tasks employees should continue performing independently, especially during the early stages of professional development.

AI literacy training should include evaluating evidence, recognising limitations, and understanding the consequences of decisions. Teams also need meaningful ways to measure whether AI improves the quality of their work, alongside any gains in speed.

One practical exercise I would recommend is occasionally asking employees to explain an AI-assisted recommendation without referring to the original output.

What assumptions informed the recommendation? What evidence supports it? Under what circumstances might it fail?

Their answers can reveal how deeply they understand the work they are presenting.

These practices require judgment about the task. There is little educational value in manually repeating routine activities that contribute nothing to learning. But there is considerable value in retaining the intellectual effort through which competence develops.

### The future belongs to people who can think about thinking

I have been thinking about what it will mean to be intelligent in a world where increasingly capable machines can perform activities once considered evidence of intelligence.

For a long time, access to information conferred an advantage. People who knew more could often do more.

The internet changed that relationship by making enormous amounts of information accessible to almost anyone with a connection.

Artificial intelligence is changing it again.

We can now interact with systems that organise information, generate explanations, analyse problems, and produce sophisticated intellectual work.

This makes certain capabilities more accessible. It also raises the value of understanding which problems deserve attention, what evidence should be trusted, and how competing considerations should inform a decision.

I suspect the future of human intelligence will depend increasingly on these abilities.

There is something ironic about building machines that can perform more cognitive work while paying insufficient attention to how human beings develop the capacity to think.

Perhaps the greatest danger is becoming so accustomed to receiving answers that we gradually lose our curiosity about how those answers were produced.

The evidence is still developing, and the long-term effects of generative AI on human cognition remain uncertain. We have studies showing productivity gains, evidence of learning benefits in some settings, and findings that raise concerns about retention, dependence, and critical thinking.

Together, they suggest that the consequences of AI depend considerably on how we use it.

As we continue integrating artificial intelligence into our lives, schools, and organisations, I think we should pay as much attention to the development of human intelligence as we do to the capabilities of our machines.

The next time you ask AI to think through a problem for you, consider whether the process could also help you become better at thinking through similar problems yourself.

That may be one of the most valuable questions we can ask in the age of artificial intelligence.
