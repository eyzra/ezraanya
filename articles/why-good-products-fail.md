Imagine spending two years developing a product. You have studied the market, invested your savings, hired competent people, and built something that works remarkably well. The product is affordable, the design is impressive, and early users have given positive feedback.

You launch it with considerable excitement, expecting customers to recognise its value.

Six months later, sales are disappointing.

Meanwhile, a competitor with what you consider an inferior product is selling thousands of units. Their customers seem satisfied, their business is growing, and you cannot understand why people keep choosing them.

It is tempting to conclude that customers don't know what is good for them.

But perhaps the more interesting question is whether you understand what customers are buying in the first place.

In 2005, Clayton Christensen, Scott Cook, and Taddy Hall published an article in *Harvard Business Review* examining why so many new products failed despite extensive market research and substantial marketing investments. They cited estimates that more than 90% of new consumer products failed, although such figures depend heavily on how failure is defined and measured.

Their central argument was that businesses frequently misunderstand the circumstances that cause people to purchase products.

Two decades later, the problem remains relevant.

Companies have access to sophisticated customer analytics, artificial intelligence, behavioural data, and increasingly precise advertising tools. Yet developing a commercially successful product remains difficult.

I think part of the explanation lies in how we understand consumer behaviour.

Businesses often evaluate their products through the qualities they have built into them. Customers evaluate those products through the circumstances of their own lives, the alternatives available to them, and the consequences they expect from choosing one option over another.

**The value a business creates and the value a customer perceives are related, but they are not necessarily the same thing.**

Understanding that difference may be one of the most important lessons in marketing.

### People buy progress, even when they are buying milkshakes

One of my favourite illustrations of consumer behaviour comes from Clayton Christensen's research into what became known as the *Jobs to Be Done* theory.

Christensen described a fast-food company trying to increase milkshake sales. Its marketing team had gathered customer information, developed demographic profiles, and asked consumers how the milkshakes could be improved.

The company made changes based on the feedback, but sales barely improved.

Researchers subsequently approached the problem differently. They observed when customers purchased milkshakes, what they were doing, and the circumstances surrounding those purchases.

They discovered something interesting.

A substantial proportion of milkshakes were purchased early in the morning by people travelling to work.

These customers wanted something that would keep them occupied during a long commute, satisfy their hunger, and be convenient to consume while driving.

The milkshake was competing with bananas, bagels, doughnuts, and other possible breakfast options.

Suddenly, the problem looked different.

The business had been thinking about how to make a better milkshake. Its customers were trying to make a boring morning commute more pleasant while managing their hunger.

Christensen described this through the idea that customers *hire* products to accomplish particular jobs.

The milkshake story is an illustrative business case rather than a controlled experiment, but its underlying insight is useful.

Consider someone buying a laptop.

The manufacturer may emphasise processor speed, storage capacity, screen resolution, and battery performance.

But a university student may be looking for a reliable device that can survive four years of school. A designer may prioritise software compatibility. A consultant may want something lightweight that looks professional during client meetings.

They are buying the same category of product for different reasons.

This is why a business can build something technically impressive and still struggle to attract customers.

It may have improved the product without adequately understanding the problem people wanted solved.

### The best product doesn't always feel like the safest choice

Imagine you need to purchase a new smartphone.

You have two options.

The first comes from a familiar brand. You have used its products before, know people who own them, and can easily find repair centres and accessories.

The second comes from a relatively unknown manufacturer. Its specifications are better, its price is lower, and its design is attractive.

Which would you choose?

Your decision may depend on how much you know about the second manufacturer, how important reliability is to you, and what might happen if the phone develops a fault.

This introduces a concept that has been studied in consumer research for decades: *perceived risk*.

Consumers make decisions under uncertainty. Before buying a product, they cannot always know whether it will perform as promised, how long it will last, or whether the seller will honour its commitments.

They therefore rely on signals that help them estimate the likelihood of a satisfactory outcome.

A familiar brand, a trusted recommendation, a credible warranty, and a history of reliable service can all influence that assessment.

This helps explain why consumers sometimes pay more for products that appear technically similar to cheaper alternatives.

The additional money may purchase something beyond the product's immediate functional features. It may reduce the uncertainty surrounding the purchase.

Think about choosing a hospital, hiring a consultant, or selecting a contractor to build your house.

In each case, the consequences of making a poor decision can be significant.

You may be willing to pay a premium for a provider with a strong reputation, credible evidence of competence, and a history of delivering satisfactory results.

For businesses, this has an important implication.

Improving the product is valuable. But customers must also have sufficient reason to believe that the product will deliver what it promises.

A technically superior offering with weak credibility may struggle against an established alternative that feels safer.

### Why familiarity has economic value

There is a psychological reason familiar brands often enjoy an advantage.

Human beings cannot carefully evaluate every available option whenever they need to make a purchase.

Imagine walking into a supermarket to buy toothpaste.

You could examine every brand, compare ingredients, read customer reviews, investigate manufacturing standards, and calculate the relative value of each product.

But you probably won't.

You may simply pick a brand you recognise, particularly if it has served you well before.

Psychologists refer to one relevant phenomenon as the *mere-exposure effect*. In a 1968 paper, Robert Zajonc demonstrated that repeated exposure to stimuli could increase people's positive evaluations of them under certain conditions.

The finding has since influenced how researchers understand familiarity, preference, and advertising.

Repeated exposure does not guarantee that someone will like or purchase a product. Negative experiences, poor quality, and other factors can outweigh familiarity.

Nevertheless, being recognised can make a brand easier to consider.

This is also relevant to the work of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science.

Researchers associated with the institute distinguish between *mental availability* and *physical availability*.

Mental availability concerns how easily a brand comes to mind in relevant buying situations. Physical availability concerns how easily customers can find and purchase it.

Consider a person who suddenly needs bottled water during a long journey.

They may have no strong emotional attachment to a particular brand. They simply want safe drinking water.

A familiar brand that is readily available at the next shop has an obvious advantage.

The implication is important for businesses investing heavily in advertising.

Marketing can help create memories and associations that make a brand easier to recall when a purchasing need arises.

But those memories must connect to situations in which people are likely to buy.

A business can spend millions creating awareness and still struggle commercially if customers cannot find its products, understand their relevance, or access them conveniently.

### The paradox of having too many choices

We often assume that giving customers more options improves their experience.

More colours, more subscription plans, more product variations, and more features should theoretically make it easier for everyone to find exactly what they want.

But consumer psychology suggests that the relationship between choice and satisfaction is more complicated.

In a famous experiment published in 2000, psychologists Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper examined how consumers responded to displays containing different numbers of gourmet jams.

One display offered six varieties, while another offered twenty-four.

The larger display attracted more initial interest, but shoppers exposed to the smaller assortment were more likely to make a purchase.

The experiment helped popularise the concept of *choice overload*: the possibility that too many options can make decisions more difficult.

However, subsequent research has produced mixed results.

A 2015 meta-analysis published in the *Journal of Consumer Psychology* examined 99 observations involving 7,202 participants. It found that the effects of assortment size depended on factors including the complexity of the choices, the difficulty of the decision, and how clearly consumers understood their preferences.

The lesson is therefore more nuanced than simply offering fewer products.

A customer who knows exactly what they want may appreciate a wide assortment. Someone unfamiliar with a product category may find the same number of options overwhelming.

Consider a software company offering eight subscription plans with overlapping features.

A customer may struggle to determine which plan provides the best value. The business might believe it is accommodating different customer needs, while the customer experiences uncertainty.

A clearer pricing structure, meaningful comparisons, and straightforward recommendations could make the decision easier.

The objective is to help customers understand their options and choose confidently.

Sometimes, making a product easier to choose can be as commercially important as improving the product itself.

### Customers compare your product with alternatives you may never have considered

Suppose you run a company that provides online professional training.

You identify other training providers as your competitors. You compare course content, instructor qualifications, pricing, and certificates.

Your programme appears superior.

Yet enrolment remains disappointing.

Perhaps the prospective customer is comparing your programme with something entirely different.

They might be considering whether to spend the money on internet access, transportation, a professional examination, or household responsibilities.

They may be deciding whether to study after work or use that time to rest.

Your product is competing for resources that customers could use in many other ways.

This is particularly important in markets where disposable income is constrained.

Consider a Nigerian professional earning ₦250,000 monthly.

A ₦75,000 training programme represents 30% of that person's monthly income.

Even if the programme is valuable, purchasing it may require postponing other important expenses.

The customer must believe that the expected benefit justifies the sacrifice.

And the sacrifice includes more than money.

Time, effort, uncertainty, and the possibility of disappointment also influence the decision.

This is one reason businesses need to investigate what customers are giving up when they purchase their products.

The relevant competition may extend far beyond companies selling similar things.

### The difference between what customers say and what they do

There is another challenge in understanding consumer behaviour.

People are not always accurate predictors of their future purchasing decisions.

Imagine conducting a survey asking whether people would pay more for environmentally sustainable packaging.

Many respondents may express a preference for sustainable products.

But when they enter a supermarket, price, convenience, availability, and established habits may influence their actual choices.

This does not necessarily mean the respondents were dishonest.

Their answers may represent genuine preferences that compete with other priorities when a real purchasing decision arises.

A person can care about sustainability and still choose the cheaper product when money is tight.

A customer can admire an innovative service and still avoid subscribing because they are uncertain about its value.

This distinction matters when businesses rely heavily on surveys to validate new products.

Asking people whether they like an idea is useful, but it provides limited evidence about whether they will purchase it.

A stronger approach involves observing behaviour.

Will customers join a waiting list? Will they pay a deposit? Will they spend time testing the product? Will they return after the first purchase? Will they recommend it to someone else?

These behaviours can reveal things that stated preferences may miss.

Of course, even behavioural indicators require careful interpretation. Someone joining a waiting list may be curious rather than committed. A customer may purchase once because of a discount and never return.

Businesses need to distinguish expressions of interest from evidence of sustained demand.

### Why marketing cannot rescue every product

I often encounter the assumption that a struggling business simply needs better marketing.

Sometimes that is true.

A business may have a valuable product that its intended customers barely know exists. Its positioning may be unclear, its distribution ineffective, or its communication inconsistent.

Improving those areas can make a meaningful difference.

But marketing also has limits.

If a product repeatedly disappoints customers, advertising may attract more people who eventually have the same disappointing experience.

If the price exceeds what customers can reasonably afford, greater awareness may produce interest without purchases.

And if the product solves a problem that customers consider unimportant, even an excellent campaign may struggle to create sustained demand.

This is why marketing strategy should begin with an understanding of the market.

Who experiences the problem? How frequently does it occur? What do people currently do about it? What are they willing and able to sacrifice for a better solution?

The answers should influence product development, pricing, distribution, and communication.

Consider a business selling a productivity application.

The founders may be excited about its advanced features, integrations, and artificial intelligence capabilities.

But users may simply want an uncomplicated way to organise their daily responsibilities.

If the product requires extensive setup and constant maintenance, the features intended to make it valuable may become obstacles to adoption.

The business could respond by increasing its advertising budget.

A more useful investigation might begin with understanding why users abandon the application after installation.

Good marketing can help people recognise value. Sustained commercial success also requires the business to deliver that value in a form customers can use.

### What businesses should do differently

If I were advising a business preparing to launch a new product, I would want to understand the customer's decision before spending heavily on promotion.

I would begin by speaking with people who have recently experienced the problem the product is supposed to solve.

Rather than asking whether they like the proposed solution, I would ask them to describe what happened the last time they encountered the problem.

What did they do? Which alternatives did they consider? How much did the situation cost them? Why did they choose their eventual solution?

These conversations can reveal motivations that conventional demographic profiles may overlook.

Next, I would investigate the obstacles preventing people from purchasing.

Perhaps customers understand the product but distrust the provider. Maybe the price is difficult to justify, or the product is unavailable through their preferred purchasing channels.

Each obstacle requires a different response.

A trust problem may require credible demonstrations, testimonials, guarantees, or a stronger record of delivery.

A convenience problem may require simpler onboarding, improved distribution, or fewer steps between interest and purchase.

A value problem may require changes to the offering itself.

Finally, I would examine what happens after customers buy.

Do they use the product? Does it solve the problem? Do they purchase again? What causes dissatisfaction?

These questions are particularly important because initial sales can conceal weaknesses in the product's long-term value.

A successful launch creates an opportunity to build a business. What happens afterward determines whether that opportunity develops into something sustainable.

### Understanding what people are really buying

I think one of the most expensive mistakes businesses make is assuming that customers evaluate products with the same information and priorities as the people who create them.

A software engineer may admire an elegant technical architecture. The customer wants the application to work reliably.

A designer may appreciate the sophistication of a visual identity. The customer wants to recognise the brand and understand what it offers.

A founder may be proud of the effort invested in developing a product. The customer is concerned with what the product will do for them.

This difference becomes especially important when businesses begin confusing their internal achievements with evidence of market demand.

The number of features developed, the sophistication of the technology, and the amount invested in production may demonstrate considerable effort.

But customers make purchasing decisions within the circumstances of their own lives.

They consider the problem they want solved, the alternatives they trust, the money they can afford to spend, and the consequences of choosing incorrectly.

And sometimes, their decisions will appear irrational until we understand the circumstances influencing them.

The challenge for businesses is to study those circumstances carefully enough to make better decisions about what they build and how they bring it to market.

Before investing in another feature, another advertising campaign, or another product variation, it may be worth spending more time understanding the last customer who considered your offering and decided against buying it.

Their decision could tell you more about your business than another hundred compliments about how good your product looks.
